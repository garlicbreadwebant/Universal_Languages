# Taluma grammar 0.1.0

## 1. Statements and noun phrases

Use subject + tense + aspect + negation + modal + verb + object + relations + adverbs. Omit unused slots. Verbs never change for person or number. A statement has an overt subject, except existential eki followed by the thing that exists. Commands omit the subject.

`mi leka lipo.` — I read a book or books.

`eki aku.` — There is water.

Use esa for identity, description, and location. Transitive entries take one direct object; recipient, instrument, topic, or location use the relations in their notes. Intransitive entries have no direct object. Do not silently change a verb's argument pattern.

Lasi (allow) takes the permitted person as its object and a plus a content clause for the permitted action: mi lasi ti a kepi ti pala nasuku means I allow you to walk. Wila (become) takes a resulting description or identity noun rather than an affected object. Wati (wait) is intransitive; po introduces what is awaited. Kausa (cause) takes a content clause describing the result.

Noun phrase order is article or demonstrative + quantity + noun + descriptions + possessive phrase. Sa introduces an unspecified referent (a/an) and defaults to one countable item when there is no quantity word; sa ma introduces an unspecified plural group. La identifies a contextually recoverable referent (the) without specifying number. Do not combine an article with ta or to. Bare nouns are number-neutral and do not assert definiteness. Ma explicitly means more than one; a cardinal replaces ma. Use una for exactly one when the distinction matters. Countability comes from the sense of the noun; do not use ma for an undivided substance.

`mi leka sa lipo.` — I read an unspecified book.

`mi leka la tuli lipo pona.` — I read the two good books.

`lipo te mi` — my book or books.

Possession uses te after the noun and its descriptions. End the noun phrase before attaching another relation; avoid nested possession by using a separate sentence. Descriptions follow nouns and apply independently. They do not acquire a plural ending.

## 2. Pronouns, reference, and questions

Mi is I/me; ti is one addressee; li is one third person or thing; mu is we/us; timu is multiple addressees; lu is they/them. Li does not specify gender. Mu does not specify whether the listener is included: say mi ni ti for I and you. Se is a reflexive object referring only to the subject of its own clause.

Use a repeated noun or a proper name when multiple people could be li. Introduce a referent with sa and reidentify it with la; articles do not override ambiguity about which referent is intended.

Append ka to a yes/no question. Question words stay where their answers belong: ki who; ke what; kana where; kama when; kimo how; kaniti how many/much; kela which; kosa why. Kosa follows the clause it questions. Do not add ka to a question already containing a question word.

`ti lena ka?` — Do you learn?

`ki sapu ti?` — Who helps you?

## 3. Time, aspect, and scope

Pa marks past and pu future. Unmarked time is present unless an explicit time expression in that same clause establishes another time. A tense marker and time expression must agree. An earlier sentence's tense does not silently carry over: repeat pa or pu in the next clause when needed.

Tu marks an ongoing event; pe marks completion before or by the reference time. Choose at most one aspect marker. For completed aspect, state the reference time when it is not clear.

`mi pa tu lena.` — I was learning.

`mi pu pe leka la lipo puti.` — I will have finished reading the book by tomorrow.

No negates the following predicate, including a modal. Pote is ability and tewa obligation. Bare modal + verb shares its subject; modal + kepi clause allows explicit scope or a different subject. Wola (want) and neta (need) can take a noun or a content clause; wola can also take a bare verb with the same subject. Do not stack modals in a simple predicate.

`mi no pote pala.` — I cannot walk.

`mi no tewa pala.` — I am not obliged to walk.

`mi tewa kepi mi no pala nasuku.` — I am obliged not to walk.

`pali no wata!` — Please do not go!

Answer negative questions with a complete affirmative or negative clause. Ya and no are safe short answers to affirmative questions; a full clause avoids differing conventions for negative questions.

## 4. Relations, modifiers, and word building

Relations precede a noun phrase: na at/in; sula on a surface; su above; nu under; a toward/to; tawe from; konu with/accompanied by; sena without; po for; pi using/by means of; mita between; pili about; luwa than. Unlike Lumena's broad su, Taluma separates contact on a surface from position above it. Nina and pola are inside and outside; use na nina te or na pola te before a noun to specify an interior or exterior.

`mi pana lipo a ti.` — I give a book to you.

`mi kata pano pi ilo.` — I cut cloth using a tool.

Degree words precede a description: pulu more, masi most, weli very, pale equally. Use luwa for a comparison standard and state the comparison group for a superlative.

Adverbs, including also, already, still, often, and together, follow the object and relations. Time phrases follow those adverbs. Solo (only) immediately precedes the phrase it restricts; it may precede a subject, object, or relation phrase. Do not put solo before a bare verb; use a content clause to restrict an action proposition.

`mi lena aniki.` — I also learn.

`solo mi lena.` — Only I learn.

`mi leka solo la lipo.` — I read only the book.

The following suffixes are productive and always written with a hyphen. Keep the root unchanged. Use one suffix per root in this core; do not chain them.

| Suffix | Input and meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ki | action verb → person who does it | lena-ki — learner |
| -mo | action verb → place intended for it | lena-mo — school / learning place |
| -po | action verb → instrument for it | siki-po — writing tool |
| -la | noun → description related to it | sola-la — solar |
| -me | description → manner adverb | pona-me — well |
| -se | description → state or quality noun | sana-se — health |

Use a direct adjective for a thing's property and -me for an event's manner. Kasi is the stem careful, defined in the carefully entry; kasi-me means carefully. The spelling checker requires every compound and derivation stem to have a definition. Do not freely convert nouns to verbs. Compounds are head first, hyphenated, and explicitly defined in the lexicon; derivation has the listed general meaning, while a specialized sense needs a dictionary note.

`mi sewa nila yapa.` — I see a fast child.

`mi sewa nila yapa-me.` — I see the child quickly.

## 5. Clauses and quotation

Ni joins clauses or phrases; o is inclusive or; wa is but; pako is because. Repeat subjects in coordinated clauses. For an exclusive alternative use an explicit choice with una (one), not o alone.

Kepi opens a content clause; nasuku closes it. Yali opens a relative clause after its head noun; moteka closes it. Inside a relative clause ye resumes the head noun in the required position. Nested clauses must close in reverse opening order. Every opened clause closes, including at the end of a sentence. These words are spoken; punctuation cannot replace them.

`mi sawa kepi ti pa wena nasuku.` — I know that you came.

`penu yali ye sapu mi moteka esa na tomo.` — The person who helps me is in the house.

Si opens a condition; nopaku closes the condition before the result clause. The condition precedes its result; do not use an unmarked trailing if-clause. For clarity, flatten sentences with several nested clauses into short statements.

`si ti wena nopaku mi pu pami nami.` — If you come, I will make food.

Kotu opens a direct quotation and pusano closes it. Pronouns and time words inside refer to the quoted speaker's context. Direct quotations do not nest in this core; report an inner quotation indirectly with kepi. There is no passive construction: name the actor or use sa penu (someone).

`li teli kotu mi wola aku pusano.` — They say, "I want water."

These speech-management words are additional grammar vocabulary within the say and clause-reference entries, not extra English dictionary headwords.

## 6. Numbers and precision

Digits and scales have the dictionary forms. Multiply a scale by its preceding digit; adjacent descending groups add. Teka una is 11; tuli teka sani is 23. This grouping is defined through 9999. For larger values or identifiers, nume begins individual digit reading. Omit zero groups in grouped numbers; retain every digit in digit reading.

Puto is the decimal separator followed by individual fractional digits. Neka before a number means minus. Suli between quantities means divided by; noli before a cardinal makes an ordinal. Specify units and calendars when relevant. Tilita is the transitive action divide, with the divisor introduced by pi; suli is specifically the numerical reading divided by.

`tuli puto suno lima` — 2.05.

`una suli tuli` — one half.

`noli sani lipo` — third book.

Distinguish nomu (numeric quantity) from sipo (a written digit). Cardinals identify quantity; an identifier such as a room number uses nume plus digits. Do not assume that an unspecified number is a particular measurement or identifying label.

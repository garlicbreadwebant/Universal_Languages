# Taluma design, evidence, and evaluation

## Purpose and inheritance

Taluma is a separate prototype, not a compatibility layer. It adopts Lumena's small sound inventory and invariant word classes, Narela's predictable derivation, and Esperanto's principle of reusable form–meaning patterns. It avoids Esperanto's consonant clusters, noun/adjective agreement, and required object-case inflection. This choice favors some learning tasks while sacrificing compactness and flexible word order.

The 397 English lookup labels match the existing collections exactly. Aliases are preserved for comparison. This does not mean the lexicon has 397 roots, every English meaning is covered, or all native-language concepts reduce to the English list. Roots include inherited forms and provisional adaptations; no systematic global recognition study or name clearance has been performed.

## Responses to the Lumena review

| Issue | Taluma design response | Remaining test |
|---|---|---|
| Article rows translated as book | Actual sa and la articles with usage examples | Referential clarity across language backgrounds |
| Undefined components such as kasi | Explicit stem definition and dependency checks on every dictionary phrase and lesson example | Human semantic review |
| Unexplained preverbal adverbs | Adverbs follow object and relations; solo has a separately defined scope rule | Comprehension of scope contrasts |
| Similar clause closers | Kepi/nasuku and yali/moteka differ in several segments and length | Mishearing rates in noise |
| Long doer and place compounds | Six regular suffixes; lena-ki and lena-mo | Retention and speech effort |
| Ambiguous surface on versus above | Sula means surface contact; su means above | Spatial instruction tasks |
| Missing quotation conventions | Kotu/pusano plus explicit pronoun/time context | Short narrative retelling |
| Same numeral word used for quantity and digit | Nomu for quantity; sipo for digit | Number transcription |
| Checks limited to spelling and freshness | Coverage, stems, derivation categories, dictionary examples, scope contrasts, and nested markers are checked | Checks still cannot prove linguistic correctness |

## Evidence and limits

[WALS vowel inventories](https://wals.info/chapter/2) reports five-vowel systems as the most frequent individual inventory size in its sample. That is a typological observation, not a measure of how many people will learn these five sounds easily. The five-vowel choice is a compromise requiring testing.

[WALS syllable structure](https://wals.info/chapter/12) documents differences in allowed consonant sequences. Open syllables remove clusters and final-consonant requirements in native words. They also reduce the number of short available forms and can increase syllable count. Taluma retains this constraint provisionally; regular derivation cannot remove every speech-length cost.

[WALS word order](https://wals.info/chapter/81) describes the broad occurrence of SOV and SVO. SVO here is one consistent teaching convention, not a universal preferred order. Fixed order sacrifices some flexibility while keeping argument roles predictable without case endings.

There is no evidence yet that Taluma is faster to learn, more intelligible, or accessible to more people than Lumena, Narela, Esperanto, or an existing contact language. A short inventory still contains unfamiliar sounds for some learners. Latin spelling, written hyphens, and English instruction are accessibility limitations rather than globally neutral choices.

## Lexical and grammatical discipline

Avoid unrelated homographs. English aliases share forms only with explicit sense notes. Keep inferrable derivations and arbitrary lexical compounds distinct. Each defined stem has a category; suffixes have one input category and one output category. Article and grammatical entries are real forms or clearly marked constructions; they are never placeholder nouns. Verb rows record a frame and a simple example.

The initial vocabulary was assembled in passes from the project's shared English list, with new lexical and grammar choices reviewed for internal consistency. Automated tests cover spelling, definitions, and selected structural contrasts. They are not an exhaustive parser or a substitute for independent speakers and linguists checking the materials.

## Test before stabilization

Recruit consenting adult learners across unrelated language families, scripts, literacy levels, prior learning experience, and accessibility needs. Pay contributors where possible. Use multilingual instruction and recordings from several speakers. Run a pilot before choosing larger sample sizes and preregister the comparison.

Give equal teaching time, matched concepts, and counterbalanced tasks across Taluma and the baselines. Measure successful information transfer, clarification requests, word confusions, phrase length, task time, immediate recall, and delayed recall. Include noisy speech, mixed-background pairs, numbers, instructions, reference tracking, negation scope, and short narratives. Report subgroup outcomes; an improved average can conceal disadvantages.

Keep vocabulary stable during a trial. Publish anonymized methods and aggregate results with consent. Revise near-confusable roots, marker choices, or instruction based on actual errors. Remove avoidable lexical length only when comprehension is preserved. A stable release needs independent semantic review, audio testing, accessible materials, and a documented community decision process.

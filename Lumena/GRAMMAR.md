# Lumena grammar 0.1

## 1. Basic clauses

The default is **subject + predicate + object + complements**. Use an overt subject in statements. Nouns and pronouns have the same form in every role; verbs never conjugate. `mi sewa ti.` = I see you. A noun alone is number-neutral: `mi sewa nila.` = I see a child or children. Use `una nila` for exactly one child and `ma nila` for more than one. Context can supply number when it is irrelevant.

Pronouns: `mi` I; `ti` you (one); `li` a third person or thing (one); `mu` we; `timu` you (more than one); `lu` they. `mu` leaves inclusion of the listener unspecified; say `mi ni ti` (I and you) or explicitly name the group when necessary. `se` is the reflexive object referring to the subject of its own clause: `li sewa se.` = They see themself. It does not refer across clause boundaries.

Use `esa` for identity, description, or location: `li esa mela.` = They are ill. `aku esa na tama.` = Water is in the house. Use `eki` for existence: `eki aku.` = There is water. Existence clauses are the exception to the overt-subject rule. There is no passive construction in this core; name the actor or use `penu` (someone).

## 2. Noun phrases

Order: **demonstrative + quantity + noun + descriptions + possessive phrase**. All slots except the noun are optional. `ta tuli puka wala te mi` = these two large books of mine. `ta` this/these; `to` that/those. These can stand alone as pronouns. There are no articles. `ma` marks plural; numbers replace it. `soma` some, `ala` all, and `poka` few occupy the quantity slot. For all of a specific group say `ala te` followed by that group; `ala te ta nila` = all of these children.

Descriptions follow nouns. Multiple descriptions apply independently to the head noun, not successively to each other. Possession uses **thing + te + possessor**: `puka te ti`. A possessive phrase reaches the end of its noun phrase; rephrase nested ownership or use separate clauses if unclear. `te` indicates a relationship, not always legal ownership. Describe the relationship explicitly where that distinction matters.

## 3. Verbs, time, aspect, and negation

Predicate order: **subject + time + aspect + no + modal + verb + object + complements**. Omit unused slots. Time is `pa` past or `pu` future. With no time marker the clause is context-dependent, not obligatorily present. Aspect is `tu` ongoing or `pe` completed relative to the reference time; choose at most one. `mi pa tu numa.` = I was eating. `mi pu pe numa.` = I will have finished eating.

Time phrases such as `nuna` now or `pati` yesterday go at the end; do not combine contradictory times without explanation. State the reference time for completed aspect when context is insufficient.

`no` negates the following predicate, including any modal. `mi no pote pala.` = I cannot walk. `mi no tewa pala.` = I am not obliged to walk. To prohibit an action, use `no pala!` = Do not walk! To state an obligation not to act, use `mi tewa pasi [mi no pala] pasu.` = I must not walk. This longer form keeps negation scope explicit.

Modals: `pote` able to; `tewa` obliged to; `wola` want to. At most one modal occurs in a simple predicate. `tewa` and `wola` may take an explicit content clause instead of a bare verb; this permits explicit scope or a different embedded subject. For other nested meanings use a content clause with a suitable main verb. `wola` can also take a noun object: `mi wola aku.` = I want water. `neta` need takes a noun or content clause. `pote` means ability; permission is expressed with `lasi` allow.

## 4. Questions and commands

Append `ka` to a complete clause for yes/no questions: `ti lena ka?` = Are you learning? Answer with a full affirmative or negative clause when a bare answer could be ambiguous, especially after negative questions. `ya` affirms the proposition just asked; `no` rejects it. For a negative question prefer `mi lena` or `mi no lena`.

Question words stay where their answers belong: `ki` who; `ke` what; `kana` where; `kama` when; `kimo` how; `kaniti` how many/much; `kosa` why. `ti sewa ki?` = Whom do you see? `ki sewa ti?` = Who sees you? Do not add `ka` to these questions. `kaniti puka` = how many books. `kela puka` = which book. `kosa` asks for the reason for the whole clause and appears last.

Commands omit the subject: `wena!` = Come! Add `pali` first for please. `no` precedes a negative command. Name the addressee separately if needed.

## 5. Relations and adverbs

Relations precede their noun phrases: `na` at/in, `su` on/above, `nu` under, `a` to/toward, `tawe` from, `konu` with/accompanied by, `sena` without, `po` for/intended for, `pi` using/by means of, `mita` between. `mi pana puka a ti.` = I give a book to you. `mi kata panu pi nala.` = I cut cloth using a tool. `su` covers above with or without contact; add a full clause using `toki` (touch) when contact matters. `mita` takes a coordinated pair: `mita mi ni ti` = between me and you.

Degree words precede descriptions: `weli wala` very large; `pulu wala` larger; `masi wala` largest. A comparison uses `luwa`: `ta tama esa pulu wala luwa to tama.` = This house is larger than that house. A superlative needs a comparison set: `masi wala na ta ma tama` = largest among these houses. Equality: `ta tama esa sami wala luwa to tama.` = This house is as large as that house.

Manner follows the predicate's object, if any, and uses `me` when an object is present: `mi tala Lumena me senu.` = I speak Lumena slowly. Without `me`, a description immediately following a noun modifies that noun: `mi sewa nila me yapa.` = I see the child quickly; `mi sewa nila yapa.` = I see a fast child. For an intransitive verb, `mi pala senu` is unambiguous. `me` can always be used for clarity.

## 6. Joining and embedding

`ni` and; `o` inclusive or; `wa` but; `pako` because. Repeat full clauses for complex coordination: `mi numa ni ti sipa.` = I eat and you drink. Exclusive or uses `una te` plus alternatives and an explicit choice context; do not assume that `o` excludes both.

`si ... , ...` marks if ... then ...; `mi pu wena si ti kesa` is not the core order. Use `si ti kesa, mi pu wena.` = If you ask, I will come. End a nested or potentially ambiguous condition with `sipu` before starting the result: `si ti kesa sipu mi pu wena.` Commas alone are not a spoken delimiter.

Use `pasi` to open and `pasu` to close a content clause: `mi sawa pasi ti pa wena pasu.` = I know that you came. Markers remain spoken; square brackets in explanations are only visual aids. A nested clause must close before its parent. This is the regular way to express thoughts, reported statements, and propositions as objects. `mi tala pasi li esa mela pasu.` = I say that they are ill. Direct quotation requires announcing a quotation in the surrounding shared language until quotation conventions are standardized.

Use `yasi ... yasu` for a relative clause after a noun phrase. Inside it, `ye` resumes the head noun in its required role: `penu yasi mi sewa ye yasu` = the person whom I see. `penu yasi ye sewa mi yasu` = the person who sees me. Exactly one `ye` belongs to each relative clause; inner relative clauses have their own. This costs syllables but avoids silent gaps and uncertain attachment. Repeat a noun or use separate clauses when pronoun reference would be unclear.

## 7. Word formation and valency

Roots have the categories listed in the dictionary. Do not freely change a noun into a verb. A description can be an adverb via `me`. Noun compounds are **head first**, written with hyphens: `tama-lena` learning house (school); `penu-lena` learning person (learner); `tama-mela` illness house (clinic). These compounds conventionally describe association; they do not mechanically encode every possible relationship. Define specialized compounds in the dictionary before depending on them in precise communication. There are no productive affixes in 0.1.

Basic verb argument patterns are in the lexicon notes: transitive verbs take a direct object; intransitive verbs do not. Use `pana` give + thing + `a` recipient. `lasi` allow + person + `a` action content clause. `mi lasi ti a pasi ti pala pasu.` = I allow you to walk. For causation say `mi pami pasi ti pala pasu.` = I cause you to walk. Avoid silently changing a verb's argument pattern to imitate English.

## 8. Numbers and precision

Digits: `suno` 0, `una` 1, `tuli` 2, `sani` 3, `peka` 4, `lima` 5, `noku` 6, `sepi` 7, `watu` 8, `yune` 9. Scales: `teka` 10, `seta` 100, `mila` 1,000. Write groups largest first; a digit before a scale multiplies it, adjacent descending groups add. A bare scale means one of that scale. `teka una` = 11; `tuli teka sani` = 23; `una seta lima teka noku` = 156; `tuli mila sani seta` = 2,300. Omit zero groups. These scale constructions cover 1–9,999; use `suno` for zero. For larger values use digit reading in this core to avoid undefined recursive scale conventions.

`nume` begins a digit-by-digit reading of an identifier or large numeral; `nume una suno tuli` = the digit sequence 102. `puto` separates an integer from decimal digits: `tuli puto suno lima` = 2.05. `neka` before a number makes it negative. `pali` already means please and is never a decimal marker. Fractions use `suli` (divided by): `una suli tuli` = one half. Ordinals use `noli` before a cardinal: `noli sani puka` = third book. A quantity precedes its noun. Measurements use number + unit; specify calendar, time zone, and units in shared standard notation where precision is needed. Monetary units and technical unit names are borrowed labels, not newly assigned native roots.
